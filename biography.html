Ancestry and migration

Allama Sayyid Imdad Hussain Kazmi al-Mashhadi (1901–1975) was an intellectual, writer and speaker. Born in Gujrat (Punjab), he was the son of Sayyid Abbas Ali Shah Kazmi al‑Mashhadi and from a line of Mūsavī–Kāẓimī Sayyids whose earlier ancestors lived in the shrine city of Mashhad in northeastern Iran. Bearing the hereditary designation Kazmi al‑Mashhadi, the family migrated from the Mashhad–Khorasan region into North India. This likely took place during the earlier Mughal period (c.1500–1650), when cities such as Delhi and Meerut served as important centres of Persianate settlement. Early Mughal rulers maintained broadly favourable relations with the Shia community, actively welcoming Iranian Shia nobles and scholars.

According to a record placed in the present family cemetery in Noorpur Sayyidan in Gujrat, Allama Imdad's ancestor, Mir ʿAbdullāh Abū’l‑Qāṣim Kazmi al‑Mashhadi - a 25th‑generation descendant of Imam Mūsā al‑Kāẓim (as) - left Kankhar‑Khera in the district of Meerut in 1064 AH (c.1654) and migrated to Gujrat (Punjab), where the family line continued. Five generations later, a major flood swept through the region and destroyed the earlier family burial ground, erasing all visible traces of the ancestral graves.

Gujrat lies within the Chenab–Jhelum basin, two Himalayan rivers known for historically violent monsoon floods. Both rivers were hydrologically volatile, prone to sudden surges and channel shifts that could erase settlements. Contemporary accounts from nearby districts describe repeated damage to villages and crossings during severe monsoons, while climate evidence from the late Little Ice Age (c. 1600–1850) shows that cooler temperatures and erratic monsoon patterns in this period produced unusually intense rainfall and major floods across the Punjab river system.

In the aftermath of the flood mentioned in the family cemetery record, Sayyid Noor Shah, together with his brothers - Imam Shah, Bahawal Shah and Hasan Shah - joined by Baba Haider Shah, undertook a search to locate the lost resting places of their forebears. During this search, two wooden burial boxes were discovered. When opened, the remains of Mir Meeran and Mir Husayni - sons of Mir Abdullah Abu’l‑Qāsim Kazmi al‑Mashhadi - were found to be intact. In accordance with Islamic practice, the bodies were again given full funeral rites before being respectfully re‑interred at the present site. Following this, the tombs of the two brothers became a place of pilgrimage, where people visited with prayers and petitions. Baba Haider Shah remained beside the graves as their custodian, and upon his death he was buried next to them. These three graves were later enclosed within a modest maqbara, within what is now the Sadat-e-Kadhimiya Cemetery. In these grounds, members of this line of Sayyids of the Kazmi al-Mashhadi family have continued to be buried until the present day.

Mughals, East India Company and Nawabs of Awadh

Allama Imdad's ancestors entered North India into a stable, Persianate imperial world defined by courtly patronage, a rich culture of scholarship, poetry and religious learning. Persian was the language of administration and high culture, and migrants from Iran were readily integrated into the Mughal elite. In 1613, Emperor Jahangir had granted the East India Company initial trading rights and within a few years, additional rights offering a footing in Mughal territories. By 1707, at the height of its expansion, Emperor Aurangzeb died and Mughal authority collapsed - a result of overextension in wars, succession disputes, revenue collapse and invasions. Continuing to misread the East India Company as a harmless merchant guild, privileges were massively extended in 1717 by Farrukhsiyar, a weakened Mughal ruler of the decline era, increasingly controlled by rising noble factions.

Beginning with the Company’s royal charter granted in 1600 by Elizabeth I, a sequence of royal charters steadily transformed the East India Company from a trading corporation with a monopoly (1600, 1609) into a quasi‑sovereign power with judicial authority (1615, 1623), and ultimately full rights to wage war, fortify settlements, and govern territory (1661–1709). These charters empowered the Company to act as a state in order to secure its trading interests. Meanwhile, powerful provincial states now emerged — Awadh, Bengal, Hyderabad, Mysore, and the Marathas. The Company exploited this fragmentation, presenting itself as a trading body while secretly using its growing private army to intimidate rulers, enforce unequal treaties, manipulate succession disputes, and manufacture charges of misrule. It combined diplomatic flattery with the implicit threat of force, ensuring that favorable “agreements” and concessions were extracted through coercion disguised as partnership.

In the wake of Mughal decline, the Nawabs of Awadh - a dynasty of Twelver-Shi’i Sayyids from Nishapur - established their rule in Faizabad and later Lucknow (c.1720–1856). This environment was overwhelmingly Akhbārī-leaning, aristocratic and characterised by a devotional religious culture centred on ritual, mourning, poetry and patronage of Imambaras. From the 16th to 18th centuries, Akhbārīsm had become prominent in Safavid Persia, whilst Akhbārī and Usūlī thought had coexisted in Najaf and Karbala. By the late 18th century, the Usūlī school - originating in 10th–11th century Iraq - gained dominance, decisively shifting hawza authority toward ijtihād and juristic reasoning. After this, Akhbārīsm survived mainly in regional pockets such as Bahrain and South Asia. Awadh’s scholars continued to work through Imambara networks, courtly patronage, and Urdu Persian literary traditions, producing a distinctive output of devotion and learning.

Lucknow devotional influences and British consolidation

Allama Imdad was connected to the Awadh–Lucknow devotional and scholarly environments through his father’s family. His grandfather, Mir Sayyid Ramzan Ali Kazmi al-Mashhadi, was active in religious circles and was known in Lucknow and Amroha for his majalis khwani (recitation at religious gatherings). He had scholarly contact with Allama Sayyid Hashmat Ali Khairullahpuri (1858–1943), an Indian Shia jurist who spent a formative period in Lahore studying Arabic and Persian, and later studied and taught in Lucknow, Iraq, Iran, and Turkey. Mir Sayyid Ramzan Ali Kazmi spent much of his life in the religious environments of North India and was referred to by the title Sultan ul Zakireen (King of Reciters).

Allama Imdad's paternal uncle, Sayyid Farzand Ali Kazmi, was among the students of Allama Hakeem Sayyid Ghulam-ul-Hasnain Kanturi (c.1832–1919), a Lucknow based scholar whose work covered classical Islamic sciences, philosophy, and medicine. Farzand Ali Kazmi studied at Kanturi’s Madressa Imania. The town of Kantur represents one of the intellectual pipelines that supplied Lucknow’s Shia scholarly culture, and its scholars influenced regions far beyond Awadh, including Punjab and Indo‑Pakistani Shia networks.

However, in 1856 the East India Company deposed the last Nawab, Wajid Ali Shah and annexed Awadh. The 1857 Uprising in Meerut followed, with a march on Delhi's Imperial Red Fort to pledge loyalty to the last figurehead Mughal Emperor, Bahadur Shah Zafar. The rebels were largely Hindu and Muslim sepoys from the East India Company's Bengal Army, who rose against British rule but the direct challenge was suppressed with overwhelming and brutal force. By 1858, it led to the exile of Bahadur Shah, abolition of the Mughal throne and the East India Company being stripped of its governing powers. Direct British Crown rule - the British Raj - was imposed, which disarmed the population, and reorganised the army and administration to prevent future united resistance. Shi‘i courtly culture declined, learning shifted toward Lucknow’s ulama and connections with Najaf and Karbala were strengthened.

Education, scholarship and literary legacy

Allama Imdad received his early education in Wazirabad, then Gujranwala, and later Lahore. He passed the Munshi Fazil, Maulvi Fazil, and Adeeb Fazil examinations from Punjab University and subsequently obtained a B.A. degree. Scholars at Jamia Nazmia, Lucknow (founded in 1890 by Ayatullah Sayyid Najmul Hasan), awarded him an honorary certificate titled Shams ul Afazil (Sun of the Most Learned). He also passed the Gurmukhi examination and obtained a Giani certificate.

Although employed in government service, he pursued religious scholarship and endeavours. He delivered sermons in Jammu & Kashmir, and in Poonch, including during the rule of Raja Jagat Dev Singh (1928–1940). The Poonch administration conferred on him the title Abul Fazl Sani (The Second Abul Fazl) and presented him with a robe of honour and a certificate. During his speaking visits to Poonch, he stayed as a guest of the royal household. He also spoke in Assam, Madras, Bengal, the Central Provinces, and the United Provinces. He received numerous ijazaat (authorisations/licenses) from scholars in Iraq, Pakistan, and India.

His literary work appeared in various Indian newspapers, and he served as editor of the Ahl-e-Sunnat magazine Sufi. In addition to Punjabi and Urdu, he was proficient in English, Arabic, and Persian. His English poems and articles were published in a London magazine Victory and the Delhi based Postal Comrade. In the late 1950s, his academic and research writings were published in the monthly journal Ma'arif e Islam, behind which he became a driving force.

Allama Imdad’s major work - his Urdu translation of the Qur’an with annotations, Tafsir ul Muttaqeen - was published by Insaf Press, Lahore in 1961. His other works include Tafsir b'il Ra’y, Tahqeeq-e-Mahdi, Al Fatima, Barakat-e-Muharram bajawab Bid‘at-e-Muharram, Tatbiq-ush-Shahadat, A‘mal-e-Wajibiya and Mu‘allim-ul-Islam. He wrote Akhlaq-ul-Ma‘sumeen, Afzaliyat-e-Sadat and a large manuscript Istiqlal-e-Haq-e-Azadari (c.1500 pages) which discusses various aspects of the rituals and practices associated with the mourning of Imam Husayn.

Idara Ma'arif-e-Islam, diaspora and international chapters

Idara Ma'arif-e-Islam was established in Lahore in the mid twentieth century as a Shia scholarly institution and publisher. Allama Imdad became an active member of the organisation and served for a period as Chief Supervisor of its monthly journal, with four years as its President. During the late 1950s to mid-1970s, the Idara’s publications and scholarly activities contributed to the formation of new international chapters in England and East Pakistan.

A report published in Ma'arif-e-Islam (Volume 7, Issue 4, p.6, July 1961 - Arbaeen Edition), records correspondence from the England Chapter to the President at the Central Office in Lahore. According to this report, perhaps the earliest public Muharram majalis and mourning commemorations in Britain were organised in Birmingham in 1961, drawing more than forty participants and visitors from other cities. The majalis were addressed by Nazir Hussain and Fida Hussain, who were touring England with the troupe of the Punjabi folk artist Muhammad Alam Lohar. Alam Lohar’s performances, including Dāstān e Karbala, played a role in introducing the narrative of Karbala into Punjabi folk culture and making it accessible to wider audiences in Pakistan and globally. The Chapter secretary sought advice on religious points and delivered majalis speeches, taking ideas from articles in the Lahore monthly. Idara Ma'arif-e-Islam (Birmingham) survives today still bearing the name of the original Lahore‑based institution, as a UK charity that was registered in 1977.

The same issue of the journal (Volume 7, Issue 4, p.8, July 1961) includes a report from the East Pakistan Chapter, Tejgaon, Dhaka. It notes that during Muharram, the Idara’s Bengali language booklet Aqaid-e-Shia (Shia Beliefs) was distributed free of charge in majalis, mosques, madrasas, and local neighbourhoods. The booklet outlined key aspects of Ja'fari belief and addressed common public misunderstandings about Shi‘ism. It provided concise information on the birth and martyrdom dates of the Fourteen Infallibles and referenced scholars of Ahl al Sunnah wal Jama‘ah and contemporaries of their respective eras. The report also mentions an atmosphere of cooperation and goodwill, harmonious relations and desire for unity and progress among the community in Dhaka.

Allama Imdad Kazmi passed away on 27 September 1975 (21 Ramadan 1395 AH) and was buried in the ancestral Sadat-e-Kadhimiya cemetery, near his family home.
