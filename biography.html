Ancestry, migration and flood

Allama Sayyid Imdad Hussain Kazmi al-Mashhadi (1901–1975) was an intellectual, writer and speaker. Born in Gujrat (Punjab), he was the son of Sayyid Abbas Ali Shah Kazmi al‑Mashhadi and from a line of Mūsavī–Kāẓimī Sayyids whose earlier ancestors lived in the shrine city of Mashhad in northeastern Iran. Bearing the hereditary designation Kazmi al‑Mashhadi, the family migrated from the Mashhad–Khorasan region into North India. This likely took place during the earlier Mughal period (c.1500–1650), when cities such as Delhi and Meerut served as important centres of Persianate settlement, long before the later Shi‘i flowering of Awadh under the Nawabs (c.1720–1856). Early Mughal rulers maintained broadly favourable relations with the Shia community, actively welcoming Iranian Shia nobles and scholars.

According to a record placed in the present family cemetery in Noorpur Sayyidan in Gujrat, Allama Imdad's ancestor, Mir ʿAbdullāh Abū’l‑Qāṣim Kazmi al‑Mashhadi - a 25th‑generation descendant of Imam Mūsā al‑Kāẓim (as) - left Kankhar‑Khera in the district of Meerut in 1064 AH (c.1654) and migrated to Gujrat (Punjab), where the family line continued. Five generations later, a major flood swept through the region and destroyed the earlier family burial ground, erasing all visible traces of the ancestral graves.

Gujrat lies within the Chenab–Jhelum basin, two Himalayan rivers known for historically violent monsoon floods. Both rivers were hydrologically volatile, prone to sudden surges and channel shifts that could erase settlements. Contemporary accounts from nearby districts describe repeated damage to villages and crossings during severe monsoons, while climate evidence from the late Little Ice Age (c. 1600–1850) shows that cooler temperatures and erratic monsoon patterns in the 1600s–1700s produced unusually intense rainfall and major floods across the Punjab river system.

In the aftermath of the flood mentioned in the family cemetery record, Sayyid Noor Shah, together with his brothers - Imam Shah, Bahawal Shah and Hasan Shah - joined by Baba Haider Shah, undertook a search to locate the lost resting places of their forebears. During this search, two wooden burial boxes were discovered. When opened, the remains of Mir Meeran and Mir Husayni - sons of Mir Abdullah Abu’l‑Qāsim Kazmi al‑Mashhadi - were found to be intact. In accordance with Islamic practice, the bodies were again given full funeral rites before being respectfully re‑interred at the present site. Following this, the tombs of the two brothers became a place of pilgrimage, where people visited with prayers and petitions. Baba Haider Shah remained beside the graves as their custodian, and upon his death he was buried next to them. These three graves were later enclosed within a modest maqbara, within what is now the Sadat-e-Kadhimiya Cemetery. In these grounds, members of this line of Sayyids of the Kazmi al-Mashhadi family have continued to be buried until the present day, including the final resting place of Allama Imdad Kazmi, near his family home.

Indo-Persian intellectual influences under the Nawabs

In North India, Awadhi Shi‘i scholarship developed within a Persianate Indo‑Islamic setting shaped by the rise of the Nawabs of Awadh, who established their rule in Faizabad and later Lucknow (c.1720–1856). The Nawabs patronised imambaras, scholars, and Urdu‑Persian literary culture. This environment long retained Akhbārī influence, even though the Usūlī school — originating in 10th–11th‑century Iraq — was much earlier in formation. From the 16th to 18th centuries, Akhbārī and Usūlī thought coexisted in Najaf and Karbala, with Akhbārīsm becoming prominent under the Safavids, until the late‑18th‑century Usūlī victory led by Wahīd Bihbahānī decisively shifted hawza authority toward ijtihād and juristic reasoning. After this, Akhbārīsm survived mainly in regional pockets such as Bahrain and South Asia, where it continued to shape devotional culture. In contrast to the increasingly formal, jurist‑centred seminaries of Najaf (and much later Qom), Awadh’s scholars worked through imambara networks, courtly patronage, and Urdu‑Persian literary traditions, producing a distinctive output of devotion and learning.

Allama Imdad Kazmi was connected to this setting through his father’s family who were active in the Awadh–Lucknow scholarly tradition. His grandfather, Mir Sayyid Ramzan Ali Kazmi al-Mashhadi, was ative in religious circles and was known in Lucknow and Amroha for his majalis khwani (recitation at religious gatherings). He had scholarly contact with Allama Sayyid Hashmat Ali Khairullahpuri (1858–1943), an Indian Shia jurist who spent a formative period in Lahore studying Arabic and Persian, and later studied and taught in Lucknow, Iraq, Iran, and Turkey. Mir Sayyid Ramzan Ali Kazmi spent much of his life in the religious environments of North India and was referred to by the title Sultan ul Zakireen (King of Reciters). Allama Imdad's paternal uncle, Sayyid Farzand Ali Kazmi, was among the students of Allama Hakeem Sayyid Ghulam-ul-Hasnain Kanturi (c.1832–1919), a Lucknow based scholar whose work covered classical Islamic sciences, philosophy, and medicine. Farzand Ali Kazmi studied at Kanturi’s Madressa Imania. The town of Kantur represents one of the intellectual pipelines that supplied Lucknow’s Shia scholarly culture, and its scholars influenced regions far beyond Awadh, including Punjab and Indo‑Pakistani Shia networks.

Early life, education, scholarship and literary works

Allama Imdad received his early education in Wazirabad, then Gujranwala, and later Lahore. He passed the Munshi Fazil, Maulvi Fazil, and Adeeb Fazil examinations from Punjab University and subsequently obtained a B.A. degree. Scholars at Jamia Nazmia, Lucknow (founded in 1890 by Ayatullah Sayyid Najmul Hasan), awarded him an honorary certificate titled Shams ul Afazil (Sun of the Most Learned). He also passed the Gurmukhi examination and obtained a Giani certificate.

Although employed in government service, he pursued religious scholarship and endeavours. He delivered sermons in Jammu & Kashmir, and in Poonch, including during the rule of Raja Jagat Dev Singh (1928–1940). The Poonch administration conferred on him the title Abul Fazl Sani (The Second Abul Fazl) and presented him with a robe of honour and a certificate. During his speaking visits to Poonch, he stayed as a guest of the royal household. He also spoke in Assam, Madras, Bengal, the Central Provinces, and the United Provinces. He received numerous ijazaat (authorisations/licenses) from scholars in Iraq, Pakistan, and India.

His literary work appeared in various Indian newspapers, and he served as editor of the Ahl-e-Sunnat magazine Sufi. In addition to Punjabi and Urdu, he was proficient in English, Arabic, and Persian. His English poems and articles were published in the London based magazine Victory and the Delhi based Postal Comrade. In the late 1950s, his academic and research writings were published in the monthly journal Ma'arif e Islam, behind which he became a driving force.

Allama Imdad’s major work - his Urdu translation of the Qur’an with annotations, Tafsir ul Muttaqeen - was published by Insaf Press, Lahore in 1961. His other works include Tafsir b'il Ra’y, Tahqeeq-e-Mahdi, Al Fatima, Barakat-e-Muharram bajawab Bid‘at-e-Muharram, Tatbiq-ush-Shahadat, A‘mal-e-Wajibiya and Mu‘allim-ul-Islam. He wrote Akhlaq-ul-Ma‘sumeen, Afzaliyat-e-Sadat and a large manuscript Istiqlal-e-Haq-e-Azadari (c.1500 pages) which discusses various aspects of the rituals and practices associated with the mourning of Imam Husayn.

Idara Ma'arif-e-Islam, diaspora and international chapters

Idara Ma'arif-e-Islam was established in Lahore in the mid twentieth century as a Shia scholarly institution and publisher. Allama Imdad Kazmi became an active member of the organisation and served for a period as Chief Supervisor of its monthly journal, with four years as its President. During the late 1950s to mid 1970s, the Idara’s publications and scholarly activities contributed to the formation of new international chapters in England and East Pakistan.

A report published in Ma'arif-e-Islam (Volume 7, Issue 4, p.8, July 1961 - Arbaeen Edition), records correspondence from the England Chapter to the President at the Central Office in Lahore. According to this report, perhaps the earliest public Muharram majalis and mourning commemorations in Britain were organised in Birmingham in 1961, drawing more than forty participants and visitors from other cities. The majalis were addressed by Nazir Hussain and Fida Hussain, who were touring England with the troupe of the Punjabi folk artist Muhammad Alam Lohar. Alam Lohar’s performances, including Dāstān e Karbala, played a role in introducing the narrative of Karbala into Punjabi folk culture and making it accessible to wider audiences in Pakistan and globally. The Chapter secretary sought advice on religious points and delivered majalis speeches, taking ideas from articles in the Lahore monthly. Idara Ma'arif-e-Islam (Birmingham, UK) survives today bearing the name of the original Lahore‑based institution, as a UK charity that was registered in 1977.

The same issue of the journal (Volume 7, Issue 4, p.10, July 1961) includes a report from the East Pakistan Chapter, Tejgaon, Dhaka. It notes that during Muharram, the Idara’s Bengali language booklet Aqaid-e-Shia (Shia Beliefs) was distributed free of charge in majalis, mosques, madrasas, and local neighbourhoods. The booklet outlined key aspects of Ja'fari belief and addressed common public misunderstandings about Shi‘ism. It also provided concise information on the birth and martyrdom dates of the Fourteen Infallibles and referenced scholars of Ahl al Sunnah wal Jama‘ah and contemporaries of their respective eras.
